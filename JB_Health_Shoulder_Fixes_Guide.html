3 shoulder fixes for the arm that won't go up

A free taster of how I coach

If your shoulder has gone stiff, catches when you reach up, or you've quietly stopped putting things on the top shelf, the problem often isn't the shoulder itself: it's what the shoulder needs from everything around it. A shoulder that goes properly overhead needs a mid-back that can straighten, a shoulder blade that glides, and the small steadying muscles doing their job. Let any of those go quiet and the joint jams, catches or aches, and you start working around it.

Here are three simple moves to start putting that right. No kit, nothing fancy, nothing you can't do on a bit of floor at home. Do them a few times a week and give them a fortnight before you judge them. This is about changing how the shoulder moves, and that takes a little repetition. One thing first: if your shoulder pain is sharp, came on after a fall, or you genuinely can't lift the arm at all, get it looked at properly before anything else. This is for the everyday stiffness, not a red flag.

1
Free up the stiff mid-back
Thread the needle

Your arm can't go cleanly overhead if the mid-back below it won't straighten and turn. Years of sitting round a desk or a wheel leave the upper back stiff, and the shoulder is left trying to make up the difference on its own. Give the mid-back its movement back and the shoulder suddenly has room to work.

Start

On all fours, hands under shoulders and knees under hips. Slide your right hand out in front a little and turn the palm up.

Movement

Thread your right arm underneath your body and out to the left, letting your right shoulder and the side of your head lower gently toward the floor as you turn. Go as far as feels easy, breathe out, pause, then reach the same arm back up and open it toward the ceiling. Follow your hand with your eyes. Repeat, then swap sides.

Focus

Keep your hips still and stacked over your knees so the turn comes from your upper back, not from rocking your body. It's the mid-back doing the rotating, not the hips swinging round.

Do6–8 each side, slow and easy. Never force the turn.

The point: A mid-back that moves freely gives the shoulder the foundation it needs. Reach up through a stiff upper back and the joint jams; free it off and the arm has somewhere to go.

2
Wake the shoulder blade up
Wall slide

Your shoulder blade is meant to glide and rotate as your arm lifts, clearing a path for the joint. When it stops moving properly, the arm and the socket start bumping into each other, and that's the catch or pinch you feel near the top of a reach. This move teaches the blade to move again.

Start

Stand with your back against a wall, feet a step out from it, knees soft. Flatten your lower back gently toward the wall. Bring your arms up against the wall like a goalpost, elbows bent, backs of the hands and elbows touching the wall.

Movement

Slowly slide your arms up the wall until they're as straight as you can get them, keeping the backs of your hands and your elbows in contact with the wall the whole way. Slide back down just as slowly, drawing your elbows toward your sides and feeling your shoulder blades pull down. That's one.

Focus

Keep the backs of your hands on the wall as long as you honestly can, and don't let your lower back arch away from it. The moment either lifts off, you've reached your range for today, so stop there. Range comes with repetition, not by forcing it.

Do8–10 reps, 2–3 rounds. Smooth and controlled, no rushing.

The point: This gets the shoulder blade gliding the way it should, so the joint has a clear path overhead. A blade that moves is a shoulder that stops catching.

3
Strengthen the deep stabilisers
Prone Y

Under the big shoulder muscles sit the small ones that steady the joint and set the blade down and back. On a well-used shoulder these are usually the quiet, weak link, so the joint feels loose or unreliable at the top of a reach. Wake them up and the shoulder starts to feel steady and dependable again.

Start

Lie face down, a folded towel under your forehead so you can breathe. Reach both arms out ahead of you on the floor at roughly 45 degrees, so your body makes a Y shape. Thumbs pointing up to the ceiling.

Movement

Gently draw your shoulder blades down and together, then lift both arms a small way off the floor, leading with the thumbs. Hold for two seconds at the top, then lower slowly. Small and controlled beats high and swinging every time.

Focus

Start the lift by setting the shoulder blades down toward your back pockets, not by yanking the arms up or cranking your neck back. Keep your forehead resting down and your neck long. The work should be felt low, around the blades, not up in the neck or the tops of the shoulders.

Do8–10 reps, 2–3 rounds. Height is nothing, control is everything.

The point: This builds the small steadying muscles that keep the joint centred and the blade set. That's the strength that turns a loose, catchy shoulder back into one you trust overhead.

A word from me

These three won't fix everything, but they'll show you something worth knowing: most aches in a well-used body aren't actually where the pain is. They're upstream, in how the body moves and which muscles have gone quiet. Fix the cause and the symptom very often quietens down on its own.
